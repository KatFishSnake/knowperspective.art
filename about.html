About

Perspective you can push around

knowperspective.art is a free study tool for artists who are learning to construct linear perspective by hand: placing a station point, a cone of vision and vanishing points on the sheet, then plotting a box from them instead of eyeballing it.

Who it is for

Illustrators, concept and comic artists, architecture and urban sketchers, and anyone working through a perspective book or course who has drawn the textbook diagram and still cannot say why each line is there. It assumes you know what a horizon and a vanishing point are. It does not assume anything else.

Why it exists

The construction has only a handful of elements: the horizon, the vertical through the centre of vision, the station point, the cone of vision, the lines to the cone, and the lines from the station point to the horizon that give you the vanishing points. Most explanations show them as a finished diagram. What they rarely show is how the elements depend on each other. Push the station point down and the vanishing points spread, the cone grows and the box flattens. Turn the box and one vanishing point comes in while the other runs off the sheet. Tilt your gaze and a third one appears.

The lab draws every line from a single projection of a real box through a real eye point, so nothing on the sheet can disagree with anything else. Move one thing and everything that depends on it moves with it. That is the fastest way we know to turn the diagram into understanding.

What it teaches

  • The sheet is a window and the station point is your eye, folded down onto the paper at the viewing distance D.
  • The horizon is your eye level. Every horizontal direction vanishes somewhere on it.
  • The cone of vision is a check, not a construction. It changes nothing in the drawing; it tells you how far from the centre you may draw before things stretch, and on paper it is how you find D.
  • The 90° belongs to the box, not to your eye. The two legs from the station point are parallel to the box's edges, and where they hit the horizon are the vanishing points.
  • One-, two- and three-point are one system. Turn the box square and you get one-point. Tilt your line of sight and you get three-point.
  • Wide-angle and telephoto are just D, and a camera lens is D plus a frame.

How to use it

  1. Start from a preset, then drag the sliders and watch the sheet and the reading panel underneath it.
  2. Hover the element list on the left to light up one line at a time and read what it is for.
  3. Turn on the plan method to see the classical construction: the plan of the box folded onto the sheet, with visual rays from the station point locating every corner.
  4. Open Advanced for camera lenses, the rhombic-prism experiment, and a button that finds D from the cone the way you would on paper.
