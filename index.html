Two-point perspective

    How the pieces fit together

    The sheet is a window, SP is your eye

    Your line of sight pierces the window at the centre of vision, CV. The distance from your eye to the window is D. The paper method folds the eye down onto the sheet: SP is drawn D below CV on the vertical through CV. Because of that fold, a plan of the object drawn on the same sheet, with the horizon as the window seen edge-on, projects correctly: a visual ray from SP to a plan corner crosses the horizon exactly where that corner sits in the drawing.

    The horizon is your eye level

    Every horizontal direction vanishes somewhere on this line. Raising your eye moves the box down the sheet relative to the horizon, and lowering it moves the box up. The construction itself, SP, cone and VPs, does not move at all.

    The cone of vision is a check, not a construction

    The eye sees comfortably within about 30° of the line of sight. On the sheet that is a circle around CV with radius D · tan(30°) ≈ 0.58 · D. Changing the cone changes nothing in the projection; it only changes how far from CV you allow yourself to draw before things look stretched to someone viewing the sheet from a normal distance. On paper it is how you find D: circle your composition, then D = r / tan(30°). The button under Advanced does exactly that.

    The 90° is the box's corner, not your eye's

    From SP draw two legs parallel to the box's two horizontal edge directions. Where they hit the horizon are VP1 and VP2. They are 90° apart because the box is rectangular. Turning the box swings both legs together: one VP comes in, the other runs out. Use a different angle and you draw a prism with that corner angle, the same view of a different object.

    One-point is two-point with the box turned square

    At 0° one leg runs along the horizon and never meets it. That VP is at infinity, so those edges stay parallel to the horizon. The other leg points straight at CV, which is where the receding edges meet. Nothing else changes.

    Three-point is a tilted line of sight

    Sliding the horizon up or down the page only changes eye height. Tilt the line of sight instead and the window tilts with it: verticals converge to VP3 at D / tan(tilt) above or below CV, and the horizon moves to D · tan(tilt) on the other side of CV. The two horizontal VPs spread apart by 1 / cos(tilt), which is why the SP folded about the horizon now sits D / cos(tilt) below it.

    Wide-angle and telephoto are just D

    Small D: SP close to the sheet, VPs close together, a small cone circle and fast convergence, the wide-angle look. Large D: convergence flattens, the circle grows, and the VPs run off the sheet, the telephoto look. The front edge of the box stays the same size because it sits on the window.

    A camera lens is D plus a frame

    The focal length is D, scaled to the sheet; the sensor is the frame; the lens's diagonal angle of view is the cone, and the frame sits inscribed in it. A 60° cone is about a 37 mm lens on full frame. 50 mm gives 47°, 24 mm gives 84°, which is why a wide lens warps boxes near the frame edge: with 84° the VPs of a 45° box fall inside the circle.

    Quick reference

    Cone of vision radius on the sheet
    r = D · tan(cone ÷ 2), so a 60° cone gives r ≈ 0.58 · D
    Viewing distance from a chosen composition circle
    D = r ÷ tan(30°) ≈ 1.73 · r
    Station point
    On the vertical through CV, D below the horizon (D ÷ cos(tilt) when the line of sight is tilted)
    Vanishing points of a box rotated θ
    VP1 = D · cot(θ) and VP2 = −D · tan(θ) from CV along the horizon; at 45° both sit at ±D
    Three-point, line of sight tilted by φ
    Horizon moves to −D · tan(φ), verticals meet at VP3 = D · cot(φ), horizontal VPs spread by 1 ÷ cos(φ)
    Camera lens
    D = focal length × frame width ÷ sensor width; cone = diagonal angle of view 2 · atan(sensor diagonal ÷ 2f); 60° ≈ 37 mm on full frame
